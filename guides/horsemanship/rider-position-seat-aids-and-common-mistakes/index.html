How can I improve my seat when riding?

In this lesson, Ken and one of his apprentices work on improving seat position by riding bridleless in a roundpen. The advice includes relaxing into the center of the saddle, placing more weight in your feet than your seat, keeping your heels down and your toes out, and visualizing your feet extending all the way to the ground.

How do I control each part of my horse's body when riding?

Ken McNabb teaches riders to control the horse's body by dividing it into four compartments: head and neck, shoulders, ribs and hindquarters. He explains that the inside rein governs direction and softness, while the outside rein controls the depth of flexion and speed.

How do my emotions and body position affect my riding?

Ken McNabb argues that to communicate clearly with a horse, a rider has to control both emotions and body position. He describes three leg positions: one near the girth that moves the shoulders, one directly under the rider that moves the ribcage, and one farther back that moves the hindquarters.

What are the most common beginner riding mistakes?

This video covers five of the most common beginner mistakes the presenter sees in the saddle. On stirrup position, riders often place the stirrup too far back on the arch of the foot and let the heels lift, which throws off balance; the correct position is the stirrup on the ball of the foot with the heel slightly down.

What is impulsion and why is it important?

Impulsion is one of the most frequently used and most frequently misunderstood terms in horsemanship, and the misunderstanding — equating impulsion with speed or energy when it is actually something distinctly different from both — leads riders to produce the wrong quality in their horses when they attempt to develop it.

Why does a rider need an independent seat?

Denny Emerson explains that a rider needs a seat that can absorb the concussion of the horse's gaits, with the core of the rider's body moving in unison with the horse. Using demonstration riders, he shows exercises for developing an independent seat so the rider can absorb shock more effectively.
