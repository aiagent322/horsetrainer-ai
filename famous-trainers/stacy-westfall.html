South China, Maine — The Beginning

A Shy Girl, a Pony Named Misty, and a Dream About Desperado

Stacy Westfall was born September 26, 1974, in South China, Maine — a rural community that is about as far from the reining horse world as a person can be. Her parents, Sherri and Biff Gliddon, were not horse professionals. But her mother Sherri had a lifelong love of horses, and when Stacy was six years old — the day after breaking her arm falling off a friend's pony — the family got their first pony, named Midnight Misty. It was her father who later gave her her first full-sized horse, a mare named Bay, as a reward for good grades. She used Bay for barrel racing.

Sherri was Stacy's only instructor until she left for college. The relationship between mother and daughter, built around horses from the beginning, shaped the way Stacy thinks about the horse-human bond — as something taught, nurtured, and built on trust rather than force. When someone asked the teenage Stacy what she wanted to do with her life, she said she wanted to ride horses. "You can't go to school for that," she said. Her father challenged her to go look. She did — and found the University of Findlay in Ohio, one of the top equestrian studies programs in the world. She enrolled, attended the All American Quarter Horse Congress on a school field trip, and watched freestyle reining for the first time.

The first time I saw freestyle reining, I was fascinated. I instantly wanted to do a freestyle dressed in a duster with my face covered and ride to the song Desperado. I was painfully shy as a child. It took me ten years to achieve that goal.

— Stacy Westfall

Ten years. She held that vision for ten years — a shy girl from Maine who saw a freestyle reining class on a field trip and decided that was exactly what she wanted to do — dressed in a duster, face covered, riding to Desperado. She did not know yet that the path would take her somewhere even more extraordinary than she imagined.

The Accident That Changed Everything

A Dropped Rein — and the Discovery of What Becomes Possible

Stacy Westfall first discovered bridleless riding not by design but by accident. During a traditional reining competition, she accidentally dropped a rein. Rather than stopping, she kept riding. The horse responded. The moment planted a question she couldn't stop asking: how far can this go? How much can a horse do when the partnership is deep enough that tack becomes optional?

She began pursuing bridleless riding methodically, building on the technical foundation she had developed at Findlay with champion trainers, combining it with the relationship-first approach her mother had instilled in her from childhood. In 2003, she entered freestyle reining for the first time — not quite the Desperado ride she had imagined at Findlay, but close. She rode Can Can Lena to Ghost Riders in the Sky (Desperado's tempo didn't quite match). She won. The NRHA Futurity Freestyle Champion title in 2003 was her first major bridleless win.

The natural next question: could she do it without a saddle too? The answer took 1,000 hours of additional training to find.

The Ride — October 2006

Bareback and Bridleless, Roxy, and Her Father

2006 All American Quarter Horse Congress Freestyle Reining Championship
2006
Stacy Westfall & Whizards Baby Doll "Roxy"
Bareback  ·  Bridleless  ·  Championship
Performed to "Live Like You Were Dying" — Tim McGraw
"I dedicated the ride to my Dad, who had died 24 days before."
Video posted February 2008 · Went viral · Ellen DeGeneres Show · March 14, 2008

In 2006, Stacy Westfall's father died unexpectedly. Twenty-four days later, she was at the All American Quarter Horse Congress with Roxy — Whizards Baby Doll — the black mare she had spent over 1,000 hours training toward the ride she had been envisioning since 2003. She could have withdrawn. She performed instead. She dedicated the ride to her father. She rode bareback and bridleless to Tim McGraw's "Live Like You Were Dying." She won.

The ride was not just technically remarkable — a full reining pattern, sliding stops, spins, lead changes, circles — performed with no bridle and no saddle on a horse who responded to the lightest shifts in weight and seat. It was the most emotionally direct athletic performance in recent equestrian history, performed by a woman whose grief was twenty-four days old. The audience understood what they were watching. The performance won the championship. It also won something larger: the lasting attention of people who would not otherwise have cared about reining at all.

What the millions of viewers who watched the viral video saw was roughly four minutes. What they did not see was the thousand hours that made it possible. 800 hours to develop the bridleless partnership to championship level; 200 more for Stacy to figure out how to ride the full reining pattern without a saddle. "Achieve a very high level of training on a loose rein for a long time," she advises anyone who wants to pursue bridleless riding. "If you don't run into problems, you haven't done it long enough. When you can fix problems without the reins, you are getting closer." The point is not magic. The point is work — and the partnership that the work reveals.

The Viral Moment & Road to the Horse

Ellen DeGeneres, a Wedding Dress, and the World's Attention

When the video of the 2006 Congress ride was posted to Stacy Westfall's website in February 2008, it spread faster than any equestrian performance before it. It reached people who had never seen a reining horse, never heard of the Congress, never thought about what a horse and rider could do together without any equipment between them. In March 2008, television host Ellen DeGeneres saw it and invited Stacy and Roxy onto her show. The Ellen appearance on March 14, 2008 introduced Westfall — and bridleless reining — to a mainstream audience of millions.

In 2006 she had also made history in another arena entirely: Road to the Horse, the prestigious colt-starting competition where trainers take an untouched young horse and work with it under tight time constraints. She was the first woman ever invited to compete. She showed up wearing a wedding dress for part of the competition. She won — becoming the first and, for many years, the only woman to win Road to the Horse. The image of the wedding dress and the colt became one of the sport's most memorable visuals.

Roxy — Whizards Baby Doll — died in 2012. The black mare who carried Westfall's most iconic performances lives on in the video that brought bridleless reining to the world. Stacy's philosophy, expressed in that ride and in everything since: "The bond is the same for me with or without the bridle. Without the bridle, other people see the bond more. But nothing changes between the horse and me."
