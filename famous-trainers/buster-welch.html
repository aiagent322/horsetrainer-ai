Early Life

Sterling City, a Mother's Death, and a Boy Who Ran to the Horses

Fay Owen "Buster" Welch was born on May 23, 1928, near Sterling City, Texas — in the raw country where the Colorado and Concho Rivers divide, north of town, on land his family had worked since before the Civil War. His Tennessean ancestry ran deep in West Texas soil. Within a month of his birth, his mother died. The children were dispersed among relatives, and Buster was raised by his grandparents, Bud and Sarah Welch, on their stock farm until his father remarried and moved the family to Midland, Texas.

Even in grade school, Buster was ungovernable in the best possible way. He skipped school to spend time at the stockyards. He ran away from home more than once — one time leading a rough old horse named Handsome Harry away quietly in the night so his family wouldn't hear the horse bawling when it bucked. At thirteen years old, he left home permanently. There was no dramatic rupture. There was simply a boy who knew where he belonged, and it was with horses and cattle.

His intense passion was for the work that called on a good horse. Though ranching was his life's goal and life's work, it could not contain him.

— AQHA tribute to Buster Welch

He landed a job with cattlemen Foy and Leonard Proctor in Midland — men who ran as many as 30,000 head of cattle a year and badly needed hands during World War II. Buster started at the bottom: breaking broncs, grubbing prickly pear, chopping wood, tending cattle. Before he turned eighteen, he had already worked three legendary Texas outfits — the Proctor operation, the Long X Ranch in Jeff Davis County, and the famed Four Sixes Ranch in Guthrie. Those ranches didn't teach from books. They taught from cattle, horses, and the West Texas ground itself.

The Making of a Champion

Marion's Girl and a 26-Year-Old World Champion

Buster began training horses while working a ranch near Las Vegas, New Mexico, and gradually built a reputation among the emerging cutting horse community that was coalescing around the National Cutting Horse Association in the late 1940s and early 1950s. He saw in cutting the perfect meeting of everything he already knew: cow sense built on thousands of hours of actual ranch work, horsemanship forged on the rough stock of the great Texas ranches, and a competitive arena where the best horse and rider won on pure merit.

His first big public notice came with Chickasha Mike, a horse that put his name in cutting circles. But it was Marion's Girl who made him. Oilman Marion Flynt of Midland watched the young Welch compete and sent his bay mare to school under him. What followed was one of the great partnerships in the sport's early history. Marion's Girl was, in Buster's own estimation, the smartest and best cutting horse he ever rode. She won the NCHA World Championship in 1954 — making Buster Welch, at age 26, the youngest world champion the sport had seen — and won it again in 1956. She was inducted into the NCHA Horse Hall of Fame in 1962. She died at nine years old, never having produced a foal, and left an irreplaceable hole.

By the age of 32, Welch had not just become a champion. He had helped invent the championship itself.

Co-Founding the Futurity

1962 — Building the Sport's Greatest Stage

In 1962, Buster Welch joined nine other competitors who each pitched in to underwrite a new competition: the NCHA Futurity, a championship exclusively for three-year-old horses that had never been shown. The first event was held in Sweetwater, Texas. It was a radical idea at the time — a test not of a finished horse but of a horse's potential and a trainer's ability to develop it from the ground up. Today the NCHA Futurity is the richest and most prestigious event in cutting, held annually in Fort Worth, drawing thousands of competitors and millions of dollars in prize money.

In that inaugural 1962 Futurity, Buster Welch won. Riding Money's Glo, he took the first title in the event he had helped create. He won the next year on Chickasha Glo. He would go on to win it five times total — a record that has never been equaled — across a span of fifteen years and five entirely different horses.

King Ranch & the Greatest Partnership

Mr San Peppy, Little Peppy, and a Legacy Cast in Bronze

In 1974, Buster Welch signed on with the King Ranch — the most famous cattle operation in American history, spanning more than 800,000 acres of South Texas. His assignment was Mr San Peppy, a sorrel stallion sired by Leo San and bred by Gordon Howell of Dallas. What followed was one of the great horse-and-rider stories in Western performance history. Under Welch, Mr San Peppy won the NCHA World Championship in 1974 and again in 1976, also claiming the AQHA World Championship in Senior Cutting that same year.

But Mr San Peppy was a prelude to something even greater. His son, Peppy San Badger — foaled in 1974 and known universally as "Little Peppy" — was sold to the King Ranch in 1977 and placed under Buster Welch's hands. Little Peppy was unlike anything Welch had ridden before. "He could be a real classy, pretty horse with lots and lots of style," Welch said, "and then he could immediately turn into a fierce working horse and hold bad, bad cows." Under the old scoring system, he had marked 220.5 — a score that announced the arrival of something historic.

Together, Welch and Little Peppy won the 1977 NCHA Futurity — Buster's record fifth — the 1978 NCHA Derby, and reached the 1980 reserve World Championship and the 1981 NCHA World Futurity finals. Peppy San Badger went on to become one of the most influential sires in the Western performance horse world, his offspring dominating cutting, reining, and cow horse competition for decades. He died in 2005 at age thirty.

The partnership between Buster Welch and Peppy San Badger is immortalized in bronze at Will Rogers Memorial Center in Fort Worth, Texas — a larger-than-life statue of Welch aboard Little Peppy, cutting a Santa Gertrudis cow in full stride. It is one of the only statues in American equestrian sport that honors a trainer by name alongside his horse. It is the right tribute for the right man.

He's one-of-a-kind. I don't think we've seen anybody else who's had the depth and ability that Buster represented in our world.

— Lindy Burch, NCHA Futurity Open Champion
Character & Legacy

Cattleman First, Champion Always

For all his records, Buster Welch's identity was never simply that of a competitor. He was a cattleman first. With his wife Sheila — herself an NCHA Non-Pro Hall of Fame inductee — he raised B Lazy W branded beef cattle in Rotan, Texas, selling to Coleman Natural Meats. The ranch, the cattle, the horses: these were not separate parts of his life. They were the same life, lived without partition.

He developed styles of saddles and tack that changed how cutting horses were trained and shown and that remain in use among most competitors today. He was generous in advice to the very people competing against him in the arena. He brought a level of class and dignity to cutting that became part of the sport's identity. Working cowboys and aspiring horse trainers came to his ranch seeking knowledge, and he gave it freely — understanding that the sport he had helped build needed the next generation as much as it needed him.

He appeared as himself in the fourth season of the television series Yellowstone — a moment of mainstream recognition for a man who had never sought it but who represented everything the Western world genuinely revered. He suffered much tragedy in his ninety-four years: the early death of his mother, the death of his wife Sheila in 2014, the loss of a son and grandchildren. He never withdrew in sadness. He stayed horseback until the end.

Buster Welch died on June 12, 2022, in Abilene, Texas, at age 94. His official career tally of $1.7 million in lifetime earnings represented only the measurable portion of his contribution. The rest is in the sport he co-founded, the horses he made, the trainers he taught, the bronze that stands in Fort Worth, and the bloodlines that run through virtually every competitive cutting horse alive today.

Achievements

The Records That Define an Era

  • Four-time NCHA Open World Champion (1954, 1956, 1974, 1976) — across more than two decades of competitive dominance
  • Five-time NCHA World Championship Futurity Champion (1962, 1963, 1966, 1971, 1977) — the all-time record, never equaled
  • Co-founder of the NCHA Futurity (1962) — the sport's richest and most prestigious event
  • Won the inaugural NCHA Futurity in 1962 on Money's Glo — the first rider to win a championship he helped create
  • NCHA Members Hall of Fame inductee — 1974
  • AQHA Hall of Fame inductee — 2004
  • Texas Cowboy Hall of Fame inductee
  • NCHA Rider Hall of Fame inductee
  • National Golden Spur Award — 2012, for outstanding contributions to the ranching and livestock industry
  • Trained and rode Mr San Peppy (King Ranch) to two NCHA World Championships and the AQHA World Championship in Senior Cutting
  • Trained and rode Peppy San Badger ("Little Peppy") to the 1977 NCHA Futurity and 1978 NCHA Derby — one of the greatest sires in Western performance history
  • $1.7 million in official career lifetime earnings
  • Honored with a bronze statue at Will Rogers Memorial Center, Fort Worth — one of the only trainer-horse statues in American equestrian sport
  • Developed saddle and tack styles still used by most cutting competitors today
  • Appeared as himself in Yellowstone, Season 4, Episode 5 — "Under a Blanket of Red"
  • Remained horseback until the end of his life at age 94
