Early Life

Oregon Roots & a Family of Horsemen

William Dorrance was born on January 19, 1906, on a cattle ranch in eastern Oregon, into a family where horses were not a hobby but the foundation of daily life. His father, William "Church" Dorrance, had carved the family homestead out of the high desert with a work team in the 1880s and maintained a working herd of up to 150 horses — animals used for ranch work and available for sale to neighboring outfits. Church Dorrance operated on a handshake and his wife Minnie's word was considered final in all things — a moral straightforwardness that would define every member of the family.

Bill was one of eight children. The amenities most families took for granted were unknown on the Oregon ranch: fresh water for the bustling household was hauled half a mile in a barrel from the well when the pipes froze. Bill and his siblings doubled and tripled up on horseback to make the two-mile ride to school. There were four brothers — Fred, Jim, Bill, and Tom — and all developed into exceptional horsemen. Among that gifted family, Tom Dorrance was often said to be the finest. But Tom was always just as quick to say the same about Bill.

The Dorrance boys came up in the Great Basin "buckaroo" tradition — a California-Spanish cattle culture that prized elegant horsemanship, the vaquero's patience in developing a bridle horse, and the art of the braided rawhide reata. It was a world away from the rushed, force-based methods that dominated much of American horse training at the time. From childhood, the Dorrance brothers absorbed a philosophy that put the horse's understanding first.

California Years

Salinas, the Ranch, and the Rawhide Room

Bill eventually settled in Salinas, California, in the central coast region south of San Francisco. There he raised his family with his wife, Marie, and continued the ranching patterns he had grown up with in Oregon: cattle on the home ranch in the mountains, hay grown in the valley. The ranch on Mt. Toro, where he lived with his son Steve and Steve's family, became a living expression of his life's philosophy. His other two sons, Billy and Dave, operated the family ranch between Hollister and Gilroy.

Bill strongly believed in the capacity of others to perpetuate the "ways of going about things" that he held dear.

— From the Bill Dorrance biography, Feel of a Horse

At the center of the ranch was what visitors called the rawhide room — a treasure house, in the words of those who were fortunate enough to spend time there. Bill's braiding career began in 1933, taught by his brother Fred, and over the following six decades he produced an astonishing body of work: 122 pairs of hobbles, 103 hackamores, 35 bosals, 44 sets of reins, 19 quirts, several hundred hondas, and 67 reatas ranging in length from 60 to 75 feet. Every piece was built from raw cowhide, cut into fine strands, and braided with a precision that placed his work in the category of functional art.

Examples of his handmade rawhide tools and museum-quality braiding were featured in Braiding Rawhide Horse Tack (Cornell Maritime Press, 1985) by Bob Woolery of Salinas — one of Bill's most accomplished students. Bill and his work also appeared in two large-format collectible books: El Buckaroo (Northland Graphics, 1995) by Ernest Morris, and Voices and Visions of the American West (Texas Monthly Press, 1986) by Barney Nelson. When he could no longer braid reatas himself, he instructed other cowboys in the art, ensuring the craft would survive him.

Philosophy & Legacy

Feel, Timing, and the Horse's Point of View

Bill Dorrance's philosophy of horsemanship was rooted in a single, deceptively simple concept: feel. Not force. Not repetition through pressure alone. But an attentive, reciprocal communication between horse and human — a willingness to understand what the horse needed, rather than demanding the horse conform to what the rider wanted. This was the quiet revolution at the heart of the Dorrance brothers' teaching, and it would reshape American horsemanship for generations.

Bill and his brother Tom were among the founding fathers of what became known as natural horsemanship — a movement that emphasized reading the horse's responses, earning trust rather than demanding submission, and developing a horse gradually through feel and release rather than force and punishment. Their influence extended broadly through the Western cattle community first, reaching men and women who worked horses every day and for whom a true partnership with their animals was essential to their livelihood.

He openly appreciated the rich blessings in his life and maintained an unshakable, subtle optimism at the forefront of his actions and thoughts about other people.

— Western Horseman TV Magazine, 1997

In 1997, producers of Western Horseman TV Magazine filmed a rare interview with Bill on the Mt. Toro ranch. He was 92 years old. The footage showed a man with superb reflexes, deft hands, and a mind as clear as the central California air. He was still saddling horses. He imparted new meaning, those who knew him said, to the very concept of human potential. He went about it all, in his words, "no different than I do every day."

His influence on fellow horsemen was deep and enduring. Ray Hunt, who worked extensively with Tom Dorrance, carried the Dorrance philosophy to clinics across North America beginning in the mid-1960s. Hunt's student Buck Brannaman became perhaps the most visible ambassador of their shared tradition. Pat Parelli, Sheila Varian, Bryan Neubert, and Randy Rieman all trace direct lineage to the Dorrance brothers' way of working with horses. The entire modern natural horsemanship movement — its clinics, its DVDs, its millions of devoted practitioners — flows ultimately from this family of Oregon ranch hands who simply believed in doing right by the horse.

Achievements & Contributions

A Life Measured in Horses, Rawhide, and Influence

  • Co-founder, along with brother Tom, of the modern natural horsemanship movement in the United States
  • Master rawhide braider — 67 reatas, 103 hackamores, 122 pairs of hobbles, and hundreds of other pieces across a 66-year career
  • Author of True Horsemanship Through Feel (with Leslie Desmond), one of the most important texts in American horsemanship literature
  • Subject of the instructional video Four Strands of Rawhide: The Making of a Reata (with Randy Rieman)
  • Featured in landmark Western Americana books El Buckaroo and Voices and Visions of the American West
  • Mentor to Randy Rieman, Bryan Neubert, and a generation of California vaquero-tradition horsemen and braiders
  • Instructor of the late brother Tom Dorrance's foundational clinics, whose students include Ray Hunt and Buck Brannaman
  • Remained actively riding and ranching on Mt. Toro until very near the end of his life at age 93
  • Widely considered among the finest ranch ropers and vaquero-tradition horsemen of the 20th century
