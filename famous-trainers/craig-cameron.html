Early Life

Native Texan — Born on the Ranch, Raised in the Rough and Tumble

Craig Cameron was born in 1949 in Texas and grew up on a family ranch, riding horses from age two. He came of age watching what he would later describe as the "rough and tumble methods" of horse breaking common in the 1950s and 1960s — the old way, where breaking a horse meant exactly that. Cameron never believed in it. Even as a boy, he recognized that the unnecessary roughness wasn't working for the horse, and that there had to be a better way.

But before horsemanship became his calling, rodeo was. From 1970 to 1984, Cameron competed at the Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association (PRCA) level, specializing in bull riding — the most physically demanding, high-consequence event in the sport. Fourteen years on the PRCA card, riding bulls for a living, built in him something that no clinic curriculum can teach: the calmness of a man who has been genuinely afraid and learned to function through it. That experience — reading dangerous animals, responding rather than reacting, staying composed when things go wrong — would later become the bedrock of his teaching.

Craig grew up watching rough and tumble methods of horse breaking and never did believe or cotton to the unnecessary rough treatment of horses. He took a slower, more natural approach — and it seemed to work.

— Craig Cameron biography

After retiring from rodeo, Cameron managed cattle operations on new properties in Giddings and later the Texas Hill Country. Working horses every day in a ranch context — not for show, but for function — he began to notice his own natural ability to communicate with horses in a way that produced results others couldn't explain. He started taking in "problem" horses from neighbors. The horses came back different. People couldn't understand how, or why. They started coming to watch. And then, as Cameron himself said, "When I started making them pay, they really started listening."

The Method

Ray Hunt, "The Cowboy's Clinician," and the Public Defender of the Horse

At the point when Cameron's local reputation was growing, he heard about Ray Hunt — a horseman who advocated with the horse and not against it. The description resonated immediately. Cameron followed in Hunt's trail, absorbing the natural horsemanship philosophy and building his own expression of it: practical, cowboy-grounded, accessible to working horse owners who had neither the time nor inclination for abstract philosophy.

He called himself "The Cowboy's Clinician" — not the philosopher's clinician or the competitor's clinician, but the working cowboy's. His approach was designed for people who used horses in real life: cattlemen, trail riders, problem-horse owners, working ranch hands. His second moniker, "The Public Defender of the Horse," reflected his advocacy stance — that horses deserved a fair system, clear communication, and a human willing to understand them before demanding they comply.

His method covers the full range of ground work through advanced riding: colt starting, reining, ranch and cattle work, trail obstacles, and problem-solving for specific behavioral issues. He places heavy emphasis on reading the horse's body language and understanding the horse's instinctual responses as a prey animal — not fighting those instincts but working with them to create a willing, confident partner. His clinics blend what he describes as "education with entertainment" — Cameron is known for his humor, his plain-spoken directness, and his ability to hold an audience while doing serious work in the arena.

His four-day clinics at the Double Horn Ranch in Bluff Dale, Texas, and at his New Mexico facility draw students from across the country. He has taught clinics across the USA, Mexico, Puerto Rico, Canada, and Europe — covering more than 80,000 miles a year for over forty weeks, a pace that rivals any clinician working today.

Double Horn Ranch & Television

Bluff Dale, Texas — Building a World-Class Home Base

In 1994, as his reputation outgrew what could be managed on the road alone, Cameron established the Double Horn Ranch — Craig Cameron Clinics for Horse Training and Horsemanship — in Bluff Dale, Texas, in the rolling cedar and live oak country of the Erath County Hill Country. The ranch became the physical center of his operation: a four-day clinic destination where students could immerse themselves in his method across multiple sessions, working with their own horses under his direct supervision.

Cameron and his wife and partner Dalene have built Double Horn Ranch into one of the most recognized horsemanship facilities in Texas. Dalene is described by everyone who has visited the ranch as its heart — managing the operations, the hospitality, and the community that has grown up around Craig's teaching. Their son Cole Cameron has become a renowned clinician and performance horse trainer in his own right — a 5× EXCA World Champion who earned the Road to the Horse championship in 2021 and reserve championship in 2023, continuing the family legacy at the highest levels of competition.

Cameron's television presence amplified his reach dramatically. His RFD-TV show Ride Smart — an award-winning program demonstrating practical horsemanship techniques — brought his teaching into living rooms and tack rooms across North America. The show won a Telly Award in 2007. He also created the Extreme Cowboy Race television series, which won a Silver Telly Award in 2006. Together, the television work made Cameron one of the most widely seen horse trainers in the country.

Extreme Cowboy Racing

Inventing a Sport — The EXCA and the All-Around Horse

Of all Craig Cameron's contributions to the horse world, the one that may prove most lasting is the sport he created from scratch: Extreme Cowboy Racing. The concept grew from Cameron's conviction that the modern horse industry had lost something essential — the all-around horse. A horse that could jump a log, work cattle, ford a creek, stand still for a shot, drag a log, cross a water hazard, and do it all willingly and without drama. Competition had specialized horses into narrow disciplines. Cameron wanted to reward versatility.

Extreme Cowboy Racing is a timed obstacle course event that tests precisely that: horse and rider moving through a series of obstacles designed to emulate what cowboys and ranchers actually do — water crossings, dragging logs, ground tying, trailer loading, traversing rough terrain, shooting. The course rewards not speed alone but the quality of the partnership: a horse that trusts its rider enough to tackle anything, and a rider skilled enough to ask clearly and give the horse confidence.

The EXCA obstacles represent the full vocabulary of real horsemanship:

Water Crossing
Trust and forward impulsion into the unknown
Log Drag
Rope skills and horse calmness under pressure
Ground Tying
Horse stands free while rider completes a task
Trailer Loading
The ultimate test of trust and confidence
Rough Terrain
Uneven ground, hills, drops — real ranch topography
Shooting
Horse remains calm with a firearm aboard — authentic ranch skill

Cameron and Dalene formed the Extreme Cowboy Association (EXCA) in 2008 as the official governing body for the sport — establishing a rulebook, a points system, regional competitions, and a path to the World Championship. EXCA has since expanded to regions across the United States, Canada, and Europe. Divisions include youth, novice, non-pro, pro, and a senior amateur division called "Ride Smart" (ages 55 and up) — ensuring the sport is accessible to riders of every age and ability level. Cameron calls it the "fastest growing equine sport," and the evidence supports the claim.

Remember the days of all-around horses? Horses that could do anything — jump a log, work cattle, pony another horse, or ford a creek? That's the type of horse these competitions reward.

— Craig Cameron on Extreme Cowboy Racing
Road to the Horse & Honours

2010 World Champion and a Life of Recognition

In 2010, Craig Cameron entered Road to the Horse — the prestigious colt-starting competition where trainers gentle and ride a completely untouched horse in less than three hours before a live audience — and won. It was the capstone of a competitive career that had already included multiple appearances in the event. The championship validated what his clinic students had known for decades: Cameron's ability to connect with a horse quickly, clearly, and effectively was among the best in the world.

The honor reflects the breadth of his reputation as a horseman and the genuine respect that has crossed cultural boundaries across his career.
