Early Life

The Runt of the Ranch — Joseph, Oregon, 1910

Tom Dorrance was born on May 11, 1910, in Enterprise, Oregon, the sixth of eight children and the youngest of the four brothers in a family of ranchers near Joseph, in the Wallowa County high country of northeastern Oregon. By his own cheerful admission, he was the runt of the family — smaller and less physically imposing than his brothers Fred, Jim, and Bill. What he lacked in size, he made up for in a quality no one could measure or teach: an almost supernatural ability to observe, feel, and understand living things.

His parents raised their children with, as Tom described it, "a mixture of loving discipline and benign neglect." The freedom to explore and experiment on the ranch gave him room to develop his own character on his own terms. From the youngest age, he was drawn to animals — sprawling among the family dogs, rubbing the soft hair of their ears "in a way that was satisfying to all concerned." Horses fascinated him in a way that seemed to go beyond the usual rancher's interest. He wasn't just learning to ride them. He was learning to read them.

I had the freedom to explore and experiment, so I could develop my own character. That could have been the foundation for me to find, as I worked with animals, that this was as important to them as it had been to me.

— Tom Dorrance

His older brother Bill — already an exceptional horseman and, one day, a master rawhide braider — recalled that the family's methods with horses in those early years were often rough by necessity. "That's the way we did it before Tom got us straightened out," Bill said once, pausing over the memory. It was a telling remark. Tom's way with horses was already something apart — quieter, more observant, more patient than the methods of even a gifted horseman's household.

Oregon Years

Rancher, 4-H Leader, and the Horse's Lawyer

Tom built his life in Oregon as a working rancher and stockman. He served as a 4-H leader, guiding young people in horsemanship with the same patient, observation-based approach he applied to his own work. In 1956 he served as president of the Wallowa County Stockgrowers Association — a mark of the respect his neighbors held for him as a practical cattleman, not just as someone gifted with horses.

Word spread, as it always does in ranch country, about the man who could do things with horses that others couldn't. He had a local radio show. Neighbors brought their problem horses. Cowboys came from surrounding counties. The reputation grew quietly, without promotion or showmanship. Tom called himself "the horse's lawyer" — because he always took the horse's side of things. That phrase, spoken without irony, contained his entire philosophy. When a horse misbehaved, refused, or became dangerous, Tom's first question was never what was wrong with the horse. It was what the horse was trying to communicate, and whether the human had been listening.

He had invented the tilt-up automatic gate — a practical invention that reflected his constant searching for solutions, for better ways of doing things. The same mind that devised a mechanical improvement for ranch work was quietly revolutionizing the relationship between horse and human.

I try to feel what the horse is feeling and operate from where the horse is.

— Tom Dorrance
Philosophy

True Unity — The Whole Horse, Physical, Mental, and Innermost

In 1960, Tom sold the Oregon ranch and set out in a travel trailer to see the country — or rather, to see its horses. For the rest of his working life he traveled throughout Oregon, California, and Nevada, working with horses on ranches, taking on the cases other trainers had given up on, and quietly building the philosophy that would reshape American horsemanship.

His approach was built on three pillars — the same three that Ray Hunt would later carry to clinics across the world:

Feel
The subtle, reciprocal conversation between horse and human — not force, but listening. Feel the horse feeling you.
Timing
The release at precisely the right moment — the signal that tells the horse it has understood. Too late, and the lesson is lost.
Balance
Of horse, of rider, of pressure and release — the physical and mental equilibrium from which willing communication becomes possible.

What set Tom apart — what made clinicians who knew him call him a genius, a holy man, a kind of earthly shaman — was the depth at which he applied these ideas. He did not approach the horse as a body to be controlled. He approached what he called "the total entity" of the horse — its physical self, its mental state, and its innermost nature, the spirit underneath the behavior. He sought what he called "true unity and willing communication": a state in which horse and human operated as a single working unit, not through the dominance of one over the other, but through genuine understanding between two living beings.

He was fond of saying that everything he knew about horses he had learned from the horse. He was too modest. The horse had the raw material, but Tom Dorrance had the patience, the observation, and the willingness to keep learning that turned raw material into revelation. At a clinic in Elko, a student once watched him observe a horse simply walking toward him across the arena — and accurately diagnose something wrong in the horse's hip before it had taken ten steps. "Tom could read a horse's mind," one witness said. "At least to Tom, the kinship seemed natural. Others of us might regard it as closer to supernatural."

Influence & Legacy

Ray Hunt, Buck Brannaman, and a World Changed

Around 1960, Tom Dorrance met a young working cowboy named Ray Hunt at a fair in Elko, Nevada. He recognized in Hunt something rare: natural talent, genuine feel for horses, and the disposition to learn. Over the following decade, Dorrance mentored Hunt in everything he knew — and Hunt, possessed of a gift for communication that Dorrance freely acknowledged he himself lacked, carried the philosophy to a global audience.

Where Tom was elliptical, intuitive, and sometimes nearly impossible for beginners to follow — his speech full of pauses, double meanings, and philosophical reaches that could leave a listener wondering for days — Ray Hunt was direct and teachable. Tom once said of Hunt that he excelled at expressing ideas that Dorrance himself could barely put into words. That was the partnership: the philosopher and the orator, each completing what the other could not do alone.

Tom avoided the limelight his brother Bill and his student Ray Hunt stepped into. He did not seek media attention, did not build a brand, did not promote himself. He worked by telephone, by personal visit, by word of mouth. Yet his name appeared in the New York Times, People Magazine, and Western Horseman — not because he courted them, but because the people he had helped could not stop talking about him.

Tom Dorrance married Margaret Green in 1966 — a California-born horsewoman who had been giving riding lessons and working with horses at the Hidden Hills Stables on the Laureles Grade when they met. Over the next three decades they worked together on ranches in Monterey, San Benito, Stanislaus, and Merced counties, before settling in Monterey County in 1995. Margaret was a competitor herself — she won the Ladies Stock Horse class at the Salinas Rodeo in 1957 — and the two were, by all accounts, a true partnership. Tom spent his final years on brother Bill's ranch near Salinas. He passed away on June 11, 2003, in Monterey, California, at age 93.

Buck Brannaman — who first encountered Tom and Ray Hunt at a clinic near Bozeman, Montana — said what many felt but found hard to say: "The things that Ray Hunt and Tom Dorrance have taught me about myself have changed my life." That was the measure of Tom Dorrance. He changed not just the way people trained horses. He changed the way they thought about themselves.

Book

True Unity: Willing Communication Between Horse and Human

Tom Dorrance published True Unity: Willing Communication Between Horse and Human in 1987, edited by Milly Hunt Porter — the same woman who had edited Ray Hunt's Think Harmony with Horses nine years earlier. The book is one of the most unusual texts in American horsemanship literature: deeply philosophical, sometimes hard to follow, and utterly irreplaceable.

It is not a how-to book. Readers looking for step-by-step exercises will be disappointed and are told so directly. What it offers instead is something rarer: a framework for thinking about the horse — an invitation to approach the animal with acceptance of its whole nature, physical, mental, and innermost. To let it happen rather than trying to make it happen. To feel the horse feeling you, and to respond from that place.

The book's central chapter presents some of Tom's students sharing how his help with their horses changed their horses' and their lives — a structural choice that speaks to his priorities. The teaching always came back to relationship. He didn't want credit. He wanted the horse to get a better deal, and the human to become someone worthy of the horse's trust.

True Unity has never gone out of print since 1987. It is read slowly, returned to repeatedly, and described by serious horsemen as a book that rewards a lifetime of rereading — each pass through its pages revealing something not seen before, because the reader has changed.
