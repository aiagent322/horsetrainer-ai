When She Won the World Championship
The Varian Family — Santa Maria, California

Born to an Extraordinary Family — and Destined to Build Her Own Legacy

Sheila Varian was born August 8, 1937, in Santa Maria, California, into a family of remarkable achievers. Her grandfather, John Osborne Varian, was a poet, philosopher, and inventor. Her uncles Russell and Sigurd Varian invented the klystron vacuum tube — a foundational technology — and went on to found what became Varian Medical Systems, one of the major technology companies of the twentieth century. The Varian family knew how to build things that lasted.

Sheila graduated from California Polytechnic State University (Cal Poly, San Luis Obispo) and began her working career as a physical education teacher at Arroyo Grande High School in San Luis Obispo County. She taught for three years. But the horses were always in the picture. She had begun working with Arabian horses, attracted by their intelligence, beauty, and — against all conventional wisdom — their potential as western performance horses. She resigned from teaching and committed entirely to horses. It was 1961. She was twenty-three years old. That same year, everything changed.

Her historic win at the 1961 Reined Cow Horse World Championships at the Cow Palace gave her iconic status in the Quarter Horse-dominated world of cow horse competition, being the first and only woman, the first amateur and the first Arabian horse to ever win that prestigious award. Ask any cowboy today if he knows of Sheila Varian, and you'll probably get a tip of the hat.

— The Arabian Magazine, on Sheila Varian's legacy

The Varian Arabians ranch near Arroyo Grande — eventually 230 acres with approximately 150 horses — became one of the most significant breeding operations in the history of the Arabian horse. Over her lifetime, Sheila bred close to 900 horses. In 2013, Arabian Horse World magazine named Varian Arabians the all-time number one breeder of both English-type and Western-type Arabian horses at USEF-sanctioned shows — an unprecedented dual distinction.

October 1961 — The Cow Palace

Fifty Horses, One Arena, and a 23-Year-Old PE Teacher Who Rewrote the Rules

1961 Open Reined Cow Horse World Championship · Cow Palace · San Francisco, California
1961

Open Reined Cow Horse World Champions · Defeating 50 horses of breeds more familiar to the working western scene
First Woman to win the Open Reined Cow Horse World Championship
First Amateur to win the Open Reined Cow Horse World Championship
First Arabian horse to win the Open Reined Cow Horse World Championship
"Even though her long career started in training horses, her accomplishments in breeding them are even greater — but it is the 1961 win that gave her legendary status across the entire horse world."

The 1961 Open Reined Cow Horse Championship at the Cow Palace in San Francisco was the domain of cowboys and their Quarter Horses — it had been since the competition began. Reined cow horse was a working western discipline: hardest of the western arts, requiring herd work, reining, and fence work, demanding a horse and rider who could read and control cattle under the eyes of exacting judges. It was not a discipline that had ever been approached by a woman competitor in the open division. It had never been won by an amateur. And it had certainly never been won by an Arabian.

Sheila Varian rode in on Ronteza — a bay Arabian mare by Witez II out of Ronna, by Faronek. Witez II had himself been imported from Poland, a horse with European bloodlines. Together, they defeated fifty other horses and took the Open championship. Varian was twenty-three years old and had been teaching high school PE three years earlier. The win was not a fluke. It was the product of meticulous training, deep horsemanship, and a mare who, in the words of one tribute, "had been a successful hackamore horse" and was ready for the moment. The cowboy community — skeptical, traditional, intensely proud of its Quarter Horses — gave Sheila Varian their respect that day. She never lost it.

The Polish Mares Behind the Iron Curtain

Three Mares from Communist Poland — The Foundation of Everything

In 1961, the same year as the Cow Palace victory, Sheila Varian accomplished something almost as remarkable in a completely different arena. She negotiated the purchase and importation of three purebred Arabian mares from Polish state-owned Arabian breeding farms — at a time when Poland was still firmly behind the Iron Curtain and international horse trade with communist countries was extraordinarily difficult. The mares were:

The Three Foundation Mares · Imported from Poland · 1961 · Behind the Iron Curtain
*Naganka
Polish Arabian mare. One of three foundation mares whose bloodlines — crossed with Bay-Abi — produced the Varian Arabians dynasty.
*Bachantka
Polish Arabian mare. The Polish state breeding programs produced horses of extraordinary quality; Varian recognized this when few Americans were looking.
*Ostroga
Polish Arabian mare. Together, the three mares established a maternal line that has produced nine generations of Varian Arabians offspring.

The acquisition of these mares was not just logistically complex — it required extraordinary foresight. The Polish state stud farms (particularly Janów Podlaski) were breeding Arabians to a standard that few American operations could match. Varian saw this when almost no one else in the American Arabian world was paying attention to the communist bloc's horses. The three mares, combined with her national champion stallion Bay-Abi, became the genetic foundation upon which Varian Arabians built its entire reputation. As of 2016, the foundation mare lines had produced nine generations of offspring.

Bay-Abi & the Bloodline Dynasty

The Foundation Sire — 189,000 Estimated Descendants

In 1959, Sheila and her mother found and purchased a two-year-old bay Arabian colt named Bay-Abi from breeder Loyd Silva. He was by Errabi out of Angyl (by *Raseyn) — a well-bred young horse with national-level potential. Sheila trained him herself. In 1962, she showed Bay-Abi to the unanimous title of US National Champion Stallion, with Top 10 honors in both western and English pleasure following. He became the foundation sire of Varian Arabians.

When Bay-Abi was crossed with the three Polish mares, the results defined decades of Arabian breeding. Three of his sons — Huckleberry Bey, Bey Shah, and Barbary — collectively sired 650 champions in halter and in every performance discipline. Huckleberry Bey was the leading sire at the large Scottsdale All Arabian Show and the US National Championship for many years. His son Desperado V became the leading sire in at least five Arabian stud books. By the time of the eighth generation, an estimated 189,000 horses are descended from Bay-Abi's original first-generation get of 441. The dynasty that a 22-year-old built from one purchased colt and three Polish mares has become one of the most influential breeding programs in the horse world.
