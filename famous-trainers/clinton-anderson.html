Early Life

Cairns, Queensland — A City Kid Who Lived for the Weekend Farm

Clinton Anderson was born and raised in Cairns, Queensland, Australia — a tropical city on the northeastern coast, not exactly cattle country. His family recognized an unusual talent for horses early and cultivated it with a deliberate investment: they bought him his first horse at age nine. In between, he lived for the weekends spent at his grandparents' farm, where his grandmother put him on her old Thoroughbred mare and gave him his first taste of what horses could be.

By age twelve he had taken up polocrosse — the Australian sport that combines polo and lacrosse on horseback, demanding exceptional horsemanship, athleticism, and teamwork. He excelled well enough to be selected for a team representing his state at the national level. The sport built in him a competitive instinct and a physical confidence on horseback that would serve him for the rest of his career.

Horses are a direct reflection of us. If you want your horse to be calmer, more respectful and more motivated — you need to become that person first.

— Clinton Anderson

At fifteen, Anderson made the decision that defined his life: he left school to pursue horses full time. This was not a retreat but a commitment — the beginning of a rigorous professional apprenticeship that would cover more ground in three years than most horsemen cover in a decade.

Australian Apprenticeships

Gordon McKinlay, Ian Francis, and 600 Wild Outback Horses

At thirteen, Anderson had already met the man who would shape the foundation of everything: Gordon McKinlay, a respected Australian horseman and clinician. Under McKinlay's expert guidance, Anderson spent two years in a demanding apprenticeship — starting and training over 600 horses, many of them wild horses from the Australian Outback. These were not polished show horses in controlled environments. They were raw, feral, and unpredictable. Anderson learned to read horses in the most direct possible school: get it wrong and get hurt.

Those 600 horses built in him something that no classroom can give: an instinctive, pre-cognitive feel for what a horse was about to do — and what to do about it. It was the McKinlay apprenticeship that gave him the groundwork principles he still uses to this day. McKinlay gave him not just a method but a way of thinking about horses that would underpin the entire Downunder Horsemanship system.

At seventeen, Anderson graduated McKinlay's program and moved on to work for Ian Francis — a three-time National Cutting Horse Association Futurity Champion and five-time National Reining Horse Association Futurity Champion. Francis gave Anderson his window into the competitive world of Western performance horses — the cutting, the reining, the cow horse disciplines that Anderson would eventually pursue at the highest levels of American competition. He then opened his own training facility in Australia at age eighteen, running a horse business and building his client base before the next chapter called him to a different continent.

The Move to America

Al Dunning, Mindy, and the Launch of Downunder Horsemanship

In 1996, Anderson came to the United States for a brief but consequential apprenticeship with Al Dunning — winner of multiple AQHA World Championships at Almosta Ranch in Scottsdale, Arizona. The exposure to the American Quarter Horse world, to Dunning's multi-discipline mastery and professional program, sharpened Anderson's competitive ambitions and confirmed his direction. He returned to Australia with new knowledge and a renewed sense of purpose.

Back home, he competed — and won. At the 1997 Australian National Reining Horse Association Futurity, he won the first-go and placed third overall on his mare Mindy. It was a strong competitive statement from a young man not yet thirty. Then in October 1997, he made the United States his permanent home, bringing Mindy with him. She would become iconic — the horse that helped him establish Downunder Horsemanship and demonstrate his method to audiences who had never seen anything quite like it.

He arrived with limited resources and unlimited determination. In 1998, he began touring the United States teaching horsemanship clinics. By 2001, what he had built was ready for something larger.

The Downunder Horsemanship Method

Respect, Communication, and a Step-by-Step System

The Downunder Horsemanship Method is built on a foundational premise: mutual respect between horse and rider, established through clear communication rather than force or intimidation. It is a structured, step-by-step system — not a loose philosophy but a teachable curriculum that takes a horse and rider through a defined progression from ground work through advanced riding.

The Method unfolds through three interconnected phases, each building the relationship from a different position and level of trust:

Phase 1
Groundwork
Foundation exercises on the ground — yielding the hindquarters, forequarters, and back, building respect for the handler's space and responsiveness to pressure and release
Phase 2
Fundamentals Under Saddle
Transferring groundwork lessons to riding — yielding to leg, stopping, backing, controlling speed and direction with confidence and consistency
Phase 3
Advancing the Partnership
Building refinement, collection, and willingness — creating a horse that is safe, consistent, and genuinely enjoyable to ride in any situation

Central to the Method is an understanding of horse psychology — specifically, why horses do what they do as prey animals, and how that knowledge can be used to communicate more effectively rather than fight against their instincts. Anderson emphasizes that the horse's behavior is always a reflection of the handler's leadership. A calmer, more respectful horse is the product of a calmer, more consistent human. The work, as he has always said, starts with the person.

Television & Competition

First on TV, First to Win Road to the Horse Twice

In 2001, Clinton Anderson became the first horsemanship clinician in history to create a made-for-TV horse training program. Downunder Horsemanship aired on RFD-TV using completely untrained horses and covering the full range of problems real horse owners face — not polished demonstrations but genuine training in real time. It quickly became the network's number one equine program.

In 2011, he launched a half-hour version of the show on Fox Sports Net, a national broadcast network reaching 80 million viewers. That same year he created DownunderHorsemanship.TV, an internet streaming site giving global access to his programs. Through DVDs, online content, the television show, and nationwide Walkabout Tours, Anderson sold over 300,000 instructional DVDs worldwide — a distribution achievement that put the Downunder Horsemanship Method into living rooms and barn tack rooms across North America and beyond.

Road to the Horse — the prestigious colt-starting competition where trainers gentle and ride a completely untouched horse in less than three hours before a live audience — is one of the most demanding tests in the horsemanship world. Anderson became the first person to win it twice in a row, in 2003 and 2005, defeating some of the best clinicians in North America in both events. It was competitive proof, in front of thousands, that the Downunder Horsemanship Method produced results under pressure.

Evolution & Competition

From Clinician to Competitor — Reined Cow Horse

In 2007, Anderson created the No Worries Club — a membership community for practitioners of his Method, offering exclusive DVDs, a quarterly publication, nationwide events, and access to his most in-depth content. It became one of the most loyal membership communities in the horsemanship world, reflecting the personal investment his students felt in the Method and in him as a teacher.

In 2018, Anderson made a significant career pivot: he stepped back from the full-time clinic circuit and shifted his primary focus to training and showing reined cow horses — competing in the three-phase discipline of rein work, cow work, and herd work at the highest levels of American Western performance sport. He sold his 250-acre ranch in Stephenville, Texas, and in 2020 relocated to a purpose-built facility in Farmington, Arkansas — a 28-stall barn with a cooled indoor arena, an obstacle course, and both cutting and square pens, strategically situated for travel to the major shows in Fort Worth and Oklahoma City.

The journey from being an unknown horse trainer in Australia to one of the most renowned clinicians in the world was not easy. But my dedication to becoming the best horseman I can be has never wavered.

— Clinton Anderson

Today, Anderson trains and shows performance horses for selected partners who co-own elite reined cow horse prospects, personally selected and trained by Anderson himself. He continues to produce in-depth horsemanship content, hosts the Uncut & Real Raw podcast, and presents Walkabout Tours across the country. The Downunder Horsemanship method continues to be taught worldwide through a network of Professional Clinicians and Method Ambassadors.
