Early Life

Not a Horse Family — Eagle Point, Oregon, and the Long Way In

Todd Bergen grew up in Eagle Point, Oregon, a small town in the Rogue River Valley of southern Oregon, nestled between the Cascade mountains and the Siskiyou Range. He was not from a horse family. There was no barn in the backyard, no father who trained, no family tradition in the show pen to inherit. What there was, by his own account, was a horse down the road at a friend's place — and Bergen's desire to get there any way he could.

He started riding as a child, competing in 4-H and moving on to breed shows — learning, in the process of showing all types of horses, an early versatility that would define his entire career. But the specific disciplines that would make him famous — reining and reined cow horse — were not part of his world until he went to work for Bob Avila.

He started competing at age eleven, turned professional at eighteen, and by age twenty had gone to work for one of the most accomplished all-around horsemen in America. It was in Avila's operation that Bergen learned the technical foundation of reining and cow horse — and where, while still listed as an assistant trainer, he would win the title that he calls the most memorable moment of his career.

What started out as a great way for a kid to travel down the road to a friend's house before he had a driver's license has become the fulfillment of a man's passion.

— Todd Bergen biography
The Breakthrough

Bob Avila's Assistant — and the 1995 NRHA Futurity

Working as an assistant trainer for Bob Avila was, by any measure, a serious education. Avila — three-time NRCHA Snaffle Bit Futurity Champion, two-time World's Greatest Horseman, 37 AQHA World titles — was widely regarded as the last great all-around horseman. His operation demanded a full curriculum: halter horses, pleasure horses, rope horses, reiners, cow horses. Bergen absorbed it all.

But it was at the 1995 NRHA Open Futurity that Bergen announced himself to the world. Still listed as Avila's assistant, he rode Todasmyluckyday (Doc O'Lena x Doc Alice) to the Open Championship — winning $100,000 and the most prestigious event in reining while technically still working for someone else. He was twenty-five years old. "My most memorable moment is winning the Reining Futurity in 1995 when I was still an assistant trainer to Bob Avila," Bergen has said consistently across his career, and the weight of that statement never diminishes. To win a $100,000 Open Futurity as an assistant is extraordinarily rare — a window into the level of horsemanship Bergen already possessed before he ever operated under his own name.

That same year, he won the NRHA Derby Open on Lean With Me (Smart Little Lena x Chexy Lady) — making 1995 the year Todd Bergen arrived. He went out on his own in 1990, initially focusing on reiners, and the wins began compiling rapidly from there.

Championship Record

Ten Times at the Top — Reining and Cow Horse

What distinguishes Todd Bergen in the record books is not just the number of major championships, but the range across two demanding disciplines. He is a ten-time NRHA and NRCHA Futurity Champion and Reserve Champion — a number that spans two completely different disciplines and reflects an extraordinarily durable competitive career. His NRHA earnings exceed $2 million. His NRCHA earnings exceed $2.7 million. Total career earnings surpass $4 million, making him an EquiStat Elite $4 Million Rider.

In 2004, Bergen became an NRHA Million Dollar Rider — and with his NRCHA earnings already over the million-dollar mark by then, he became the first rider in history to achieve Million Dollar status in two different associations simultaneously. He is the sixth NRCHA Million Dollar Rider. He has since crossed $2 million in both associations, a record that remains virtually without precedent in the combined reining and reined cow horse world.

Philosophy & Passion

Cow Horses, Mental Preparation, and the Humble Pursuit of More

Despite accumulating over $2 million in reining earnings — among the highest totals in the discipline — Bergen has consistently said that his true passion lies with the reined cow horses and cutters. When he won the NRCHA Snaffle Bit Futurity in 2001 on Boonlight Dancer, it opened a door he had been looking for. "Winning in '01 just really pushed me and it made me want to do the cow horse more," he said. "It definitely gave me the bug to keep doing it and learning more about it." Ten years later, he came back to win it again on Shiners Dun Juan — a decade of refinement distilled into a second Futurity title in the same arena.

His operation has been repeatedly called "the launching pad for champions" — a description earned through the quality of professional trainers who passed through his barn. Corey Cushing, who went on to become the third NRCHA Two Million Dollar Rider in history and earned the title of youngest NRCHA Million Dollar honoree in 2012, credits Bergen as a key influence. Bergen introduced Cushing to working cow horses alongside John Slack, creating the foundation for one of the next generation's dominant careers.

I think there's a difference between being anxious and having adrenaline going and being nervous. Nerves come from uncertainty, and uncertainty comes from a lack of preparation.

— Todd Bergen on mental preparation

Bergen's philosophy of mental preparation has become one of the most discussed aspects of his career. He speaks directly about the psychological demands of elite competition — the pressure that comes not from being an unknown but from being the favorite, the way a big lead can become its own burden, the discipline required to compete only against yourself. "I've always tried to just worry about myself," he has said, "and not be unduly influenced by others at a horse show." He watched only people he knew and respected. He understood that adrenaline and nerves are different things — and that preparation is the antidote to the one he didn't want.

His humility is equally remarked upon. Despite career earnings that place him among the all-time leaders in two major associations, Bergen has consistently maintained that he has weaknesses in the cow horse, that there is always more to learn, always a better way to do it. "To this day I still feel like I have a lot of weaknesses in the cow horse," he has said. "I know a lot of guys that would laugh at that. Just to me, my personal feeling is that I feel like there is a ton of stuff that I need to learn and I need to do better."
