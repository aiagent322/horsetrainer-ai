Early Life

Child of the Depression, Son of Idaho's High Desert

Ray Hunt was born on August 31, 1929, in Paul, Idaho — a small farming community about four miles north of Burley, in the Snake River Plain. His parents, Joel Eugene Hunt and Zua Stella Shangle Hunt, soon moved the family to Mountain Home, Idaho, where Ray grew up as a child of the Great Depression. The years were hard and the work was harder: grinding farm labor, scarce resources, and the kind of daily physical demands that shaped men who were both tough and quietly resourceful.

His father raised work horses and hired them out to farms and ranches across the region. Ray learned to ride bareback, the way working people rode — necessity as the mother of horsemanship. From those early bareback miles came a sense of balance and feel that no formal instruction could replicate. He dreamed of becoming a cowboy, and when he was twenty years old, he got his first real chance.

My goal with the horse is not to beat someone; it's to win within myself.

— Ray Hunt

The famous TS Ranch in northern Nevada effectively gave the young Hunt a tryout in the spring of his twentieth year, and he landed the job despite having limited formal ranch experience. Another hand warned him that the hard work had just begun. Ray took it seriously. Those Nevada years — working cattle across the Great Basin's wide-open geography, riding green horses and problem horses and horses that nobody else wanted — formed the practical foundation of everything that would follow. The Nevada range didn't ask for theory. It asked for results.

The Meeting That Changed Everything

Tom Dorrance and Elko, Nevada, circa 1960

Around 1960, Ray Hunt met Tom Dorrance at a fair in Elko, Nevada. It was one of the most consequential encounters in the history of American horsemanship. Tom Dorrance — older, quieter, and working in an almost mystical register of communication with horses — saw in Hunt a young man with exceptional feel, natural talent, and the humility to learn. Hunt, in turn, found in Dorrance a door to a way of thinking about horses that answered questions he hadn't yet known how to ask.

Tom Dorrance was already known among serious horsemen for his ability to work from the horse's point of view — to observe what the horse needed rather than demand what the rider wanted. The meeting set Hunt on a decade of learning, questioning, and absorbing Dorrance's philosophy. In those years, Ray Hunt described himself as Dorrance's most earnest student. He would later acknowledge freely, and with characteristic generosity, that everything he became as a horseman was rooted in what Tom Dorrance had opened for him.

But Hunt was more than a vessel for another man's ideas. He possessed something Dorrance himself acknowledged: the ability to translate. Where Dorrance was elliptical, poetic, and sometimes impossible for beginners to follow, Hunt was direct. He could put into plain working cowboy language what Dorrance expressed in near-philosophical terms. He could stand in an arena with thirty horses and thirty riders — most of them struggling, many of them skeptical — and reach them. This gift of communication would define his life's work.

The things that Ray Hunt and Tom Dorrance have taught me about myself have changed my life.

— Buck Brannaman
The Clinic Years

The Man Who Invented the Horsemanship Clinic

By the early 1970s, Ray Hunt had arrived at a conclusion that would reshape his entire approach: fixing horses wasn't enough. The horses he worked with went home to the same riders who had created their problems in the first place. A lasting solution required fixing the rider too — getting humans to understand and genuinely respect what the horse needed. Around 1971, the volume of demand for his teaching, combined with this realization, led Hunt into the full-time clinic business.

He is widely credited as the originator of the modern horsemanship clinic format — the group school in which riders bring their own horses, the clinician works each horse-and-rider pair while the group observes, and understanding is built collectively rather than in isolation. Before Ray Hunt, this format didn't exist in the horse world. He built it from the ground up, working in whatever facilities were available: empty spud cellars, summer ice arenas, rodeo grounds in the pouring rain. The venue was irrelevant. The work was the thing.

Hunt opened each clinic the same way, with words that became his signature: "I'm here for the horse, to help him get a better deal." That statement, simple and direct, contained his entire philosophy. The horse was not an object to be mastered or a machine to be programmed. The horse was a partner whose experience of the human mattered — who deserved to be treated with thought and awareness rather than force and dominance.

For nearly four decades, Hunt ran clinics across the United States and internationally. His wife, Carolyn, traveled with him — a constant partner in a life lived largely on the road. By his mid-seventies, with only one functioning lung and a body increasingly challenged by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, he was still teaching. Still traveling. Still turning down more clinic requests than he could possibly fulfill. His students had turned into a global community.

He was recognized formally: inducted into the California Reined Cow Horse Hall of Fame in 2004, named the first-ever Western Horseman of the Year in 2005. But Hunt had little use for the clinician identity as an end in itself. He was known to say he didn't want to be remembered as a clinician, and he had little regard for most who called themselves that. He was a horseman who happened to teach — never the reverse.

Philosophy & Book

Think Harmony with Horses — the Gospel in Print

In 1978, Ray Hunt published Think Harmony with Horses: An In-Depth Study of Horse/Man Relationship, edited by Milly Hunt Porter. The book was assembled from hours of listening to Hunt at clinics — his spoken ideas, his answers to students, his philosophy expressed in the cadences of a working horseman rather than an academic. The result was a text unlike anything that had preceded it in American horsemanship literature.

The book is structured in three parts: Hunt's own statement of philosophy, a question-and-answer section drawn from clinic interactions, and an interpretation of the Hunt method by Vincent Carpenter, a student who attended clinics and described what actually happened in the arena. Together they give the reader something rare — not just the theory but the practice, not just the principle but the moment.

The central idea runs through every page: when you ask your horse to do something, it should be his idea. He wants to do it. He understands how to do it. He does it. That goal — voluntary, understanding-based compliance rather than coerced submission — was the north star of Hunt's entire philosophy. He made the wrong things difficult and the right things easy. He worked to unite horse and rider into a single working unit of mind and body. And he believed that every horse a person rode could be their friend — if the person had the sense, the patience, and the feel to ask for it properly.

Think Harmony with Horses went through four editions, remained continuously in print for decades, and is still considered essential reading for anyone serious about understanding the tradition that gave birth to modern natural horsemanship.

My belief in life is that we can all get along together if we try to understand one another. Every horse you ride can be your friend — because you ask this of them.

— Ray Hunt, Think Harmony with Horses (1978)
Legacy

The Source of the Modern Clinic World

Ray Hunt was not the first person to work gently with horses. Tom Dorrance preceded him. Bill Dorrance preceded him. The vaquero tradition preceded all of them. But Ray Hunt was, as Western Horseman wrote, "the man responsible for spreading the gospel of modern training and horsemanship techniques literally around the world." He was the source of the modern genre of horse clinicians — the person who invented the format that Buck Brannaman, Pat Parelli, Clinton Anderson, John Lyons, and dozens of others would inhabit after him.

Pat Parelli met Hunt in 1983 at the California Livestock Symposium. He described Tom Dorrance, Ray Hunt, and Ronnie Willis as the three masters of horsemanship who became his mentors, and he later hosted several of Hunt's clinics. When Hunt died, Parelli said: "He leaves a large legacy of compassion for horses, and truly helped change the way people think about being with a horse." Buck Brannaman — perhaps Hunt's most prominent student — has said that what Hunt and Dorrance taught him about himself changed his life entirely. Those are not small words from men not given to small words.

Hunt was also, quietly, a bridge between disciplines. At the Horseshoe Ranch in the early 1970s, he was helping 4-H kids with horsemanship in Lamoille, Nevada, while simultaneously working with jumping horses in California — turning on the barn lights after supper to work through the problem horses other trainers had given up on. His reach was wide even when his fame was still local. By the time the wider world caught up to what he had been doing for thirty years, his students had already dispersed into every corner of the horse world.

Ray Hunt passed away on March 12, 2009, in Denton, Texas, at age 79, after a long battle with COPD. He remained active in teaching into his mid-seventies despite his diminishing health — still on the road, still in the arena, still chasing the same thing he'd been chasing since he was a young man working bareback horses in Idaho. A better deal for the horse.

Achievements

A Life Measured in Changed Minds

  • Originator of the modern horsemanship clinic format — the format that defines the entire industry today
  • Tom Dorrance's best-known and most influential student, carrying his mentor's philosophy to a global audience
  • Mentor to Buck Brannaman, Pat Parelli, and a generation of the world's most prominent natural horsemanship clinicians
  • Author of Think Harmony with Horses (1978) — four editions, continuously in print, a foundational text of the natural horsemanship tradition
  • Inducted into the California Reined Cow Horse Hall of Fame — 2004
  • First-ever Western Horseman of the Year — 2005
  • Ran clinics continuously for nearly 40 years, across the United States and internationally
  • Produced multiple instructional videos: Turning Loose with Ray Hunt (1992), Colt Starting with Ray Hunt (1996), The Fort Worth Benefit with Ray Hunt (2001), and others
  • Worked with horses and riders until near the end of his life at age 79, despite severe COPD
  • Credited by Western Horseman as "the source of the modern genre of horse clinicians"
