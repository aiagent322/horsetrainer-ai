Early Life

Born in the Saddle — Salinas, California, 1935

Marvin Earl "Monty" Roberts was born on May 14, 1935, in Salinas, California, the son of a horse trainer. He grew up on the grounds of the Salinas Rodeo, California's largest equestrian event, where horses were as constant a presence as furniture. His mother taught riding students and he rode in front of her before he could walk on his own. He won his first competition at age four — on a horse named Ginger — and the life was set from that moment.

His father trained horses in the traditional way: six thousand years of methods handed down generationally, often violent, rarely fair to the animal. The horse was something to be broken, its spirit subdued rather than its trust earned. Young Monty watched and recoiled. He would later describe vowing, even as a child, to find better methods — for the horses he loved and for himself.

At age eight, Hollywood came calling. Monty served as Elizabeth Taylor's riding double in the film National Velvet (1944), the beginning of a parallel life in the film industry that would see him work as a stunt rider for Charlton Heston, Roddy McDowell, James Dean, and James Coburn. In 1955, when James Dean was sent to the Roberts family training facility by the producers of East of Eden, Dean and the young Roberts became friends; Roberts and his future wife Pat played extras in the film.

I was born in the saddle, first riding in front of my mother as she taught students to ride. I won my first competition at age four on Ginger. My father trained horses the traditional way — 6,000 years of methods handed down generationally, often violent and rarely fair to the horse. I vowed to seek better methods.

— Monty Roberts
The Discovery

Thirteen Years Old, Flat on His Belly in the Nevada Desert

At thirteen, Monty Roberts was sent to the Nevada desert to round up wild mustangs for the Salinas Rodeo Association's Wild Horse Race. What happened there changed everything — not just for Roberts, but for millions of horses and their humans across the next seven decades.

Rather than simply running the horses in, Roberts lay flat on his belly in the desert, binoculars in hand, and watched. He watched for hours. And what he saw was language. The wild horses communicated with each other through a discernible, consistent, and predictable body language — postures and gestures that conveyed fear and safety, submission and invitation, aggression and acceptance. They were talking to each other all the time. Nobody had been listening.

Roberts understood immediately that if a human could learn to speak this language — which he would later name "Equus" — the entire premise of horse training would be inverted. Instead of forcing a horse to submit through pain, coercion, and the breaking of its spirit, a person could communicate with the horse on the horse's own terms, earning its trust rather than demanding its obedience. The horse would choose to be with the human. That was the moment that became Join-Up.

He returned home and began testing his ideas quietly, at the family stable, away from his father's scrutiny. The results were extraordinary. Within minutes — not days or weeks — he could achieve a willing partnership with a horse that traditional methods might take months of traumatic work to produce. He kept refining, kept learning, kept listening. For decades he worked in relative obscurity, his methods spreading quietly through word of mouth among the horsemen and cattlemen who watched him work.

Flag Is Up Farms

The Santa Ynez Valley — Racing, Family, and 47 Foster Children

In 1966, Monty and his wife Pat — a sculptor whose artwork has always filled their home — established Flag Is Up Farms on 154 acres in the Santa Ynez Valley near Solvang, California. The farm became the foundation of a thoroughbred racing career of remarkable scope and duration. Roberts trained champion after champion, including the legendary Alleged, named Horse of the World in 1977 and 1978. He became one of the leading consignors to the Hollywood Park Two-Year-Old Thoroughbreds in Training Sale, holding that position for eighteen years.

Over seventeen years, Roberts and his team purchased and sold 195 yearlings for a total profit of over $4 million — a record of horse selection and training preparation that became globally recognized in the Thoroughbred world. His methods, applied with equal conviction on the track as in the round pen, produced results that made skeptics into believers. Articles began to be written. Leaders in the racing industry began to ask how he did it.

While building one of California's most successful horse operations, Monty and Pat were also building something quieter and more personal. In addition to their three children — Deborah, Laurel, and Marty — Pat and Monty took in 47 foster children over the years. Many of those children still return to Flag Is Up Farms. One foster son, Larry LaGue, credits his career as a jockey and his 2,400 race wins to the care and mentoring he received from Pat and Monty. Another, Satish Seemar, became the long-time Thoroughbred trainer for Sheikh Mohammed Bin Rashid al Maktoum.

Join-Up & the Queen

Windsor Castle, Queen Elizabeth II, and a Book That Changed the World

By the 1980s, word of Roberts's extraordinary work with horses had traveled beyond California, across the Atlantic, and into the stables of Buckingham Palace. The call that came from the offices of Queen Elizabeth II was, by Roberts's own telling, the moment that changed the direction of his life forever. The Queen — an avid horsewoman of lifelong passion and considerable expertise — had heard about his methods and invited him to England to demonstrate Join-Up to her staff.

He went. The Queen watched. She was, in Roberts's words, "the wind beneath my wings." She became the patron of Join-Up International. She and Roberts maintained a relationship spanning decades — over 200 telephone conversations, numerous personal visits, a mutual devotion to horses and to the idea that partnership built on trust was more powerful than submission imposed by force.

In no way could I have reached the entire world without the endorsement of Queen Elizabeth II. She was the wind beneath my wings.

— Monty Roberts on Queen Elizabeth II

Queen Elizabeth urged Roberts to write a book. That book became The Man Who Listens to Horses, published in 1996. It became a phenomenon — a New York Times bestseller translated into dozens of languages, one of three Roberts books to reach that list. It introduced Join-Up and the concept of the "language of Equus" to a mainstream reading public that had never thought deeply about what a horse was experiencing. In 2011, Queen Elizabeth appointed Roberts an Honorary Member of the Royal Victorian Order (MVO) — a personal gift of the sovereign, not requiring ministerial countersignature.

Roberts was appointed an MVO in the 2011 Birthday Honours, and the Queen served as Patron of Join-Up International until her death in 2022. In June 2012, she attended the Guards Polo Club and presented awards alongside Roberts to international polo trainers for their work promoting non-violent horse training.

The Method

Join-Up — The Language of Equus in Practice

Join-Up is not a trick, a technique, or a shortcut. It is the application of the horse's own language — Equus — to create a willing partnership based on communication rather than coercion. It typically unfolds in a round pen, using body language, eye contact, and the removal of pressure as the reward. The process can take as little as twenty to thirty minutes with an untouched horse. The result is a horse that chooses to be with the human — not because it has been forced to submit, but because it has been invited to trust.

1
Advance
The trainer enters the round pen and uses body language and eye contact to send the horse away — mimicking how a dominant horse drives another from the herd. The horse moves along the fence.
2
Read the Signals
The trainer watches for the horse's "asking" signals in Equus — inside ear locking on, head lowering, licking and chewing, an arced path that shortens the distance. The horse is communicating a request for negotiation.
3
Retreat
When the horse gives the right signals, the trainer drops eye contact, turns their shoulder away, and becomes non-threatening — the invitation for the horse to approach. This is the critical moment of trust.
4
Join-Up
The horse walks in, touches its nose to the trainer's shoulder, and follows the trainer wherever they go — freely, without a lead rope. The horse has chosen partnership. This is Join-Up.
5
Follow-Up
The trainer advances again to confirm the horse will yield its space, then retreats to confirm the invitation holds. The Join-Up is reinforced and the foundation of training begins from a place of willing trust.

Roberts has demonstrated Join-Up to audiences in dozens of countries, with horses he has never seen before, completing the process in the time it takes most trainers to halter a horse. He has won 11 world championships in the show ring using these same non-violent principles — proof, as he has always argued, that gentleness and effectiveness are not in tension but the same thing.

Books & Global Reach

Three New York Times Bestsellers and a Global Movement

Monty Roberts has put three books on the New York Times bestseller list — a crossover achievement almost unheard of for a horse trainer. His writing has reached readers who have never touched a horse and never will, because his books are not really just about horses. They are about communication, trust, leadership, and the possibility of relationship without violence — ideas that resonate across every context in which humans interact with other beings.

Roberts has been awarded an honorary doctorate from the University of Zurich. He has received the British Horse Society Appreciation Award. He speaks at international institutions and corporations worldwide — General Electric, Pfizer, JP Morgan, BMW, Toyota, Volkswagen, the International Red Cross — carrying his message that "violence is never the answer" into boardrooms and classrooms as readily as round pens. He runs a Monty Roberts International Learning Center at Flag Is Up Farms in Solvang and an online university that reaches students in dozens of countries.

Olympic show jumper Will Simpson and Olympic dressage champion Charlotte Dujardin have both cited Roberts's influence on their approach to horses. Two-time World's Greatest Horseman winner Ron Ralls has used Roberts's methods as the foundation of his training work. The reach of Join-Up has touched every discipline, every country, and every level of horsemanship — from the rank beginner in a weekend clinic to the gold medal rider on the world stage.
