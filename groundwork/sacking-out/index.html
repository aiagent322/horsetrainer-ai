Sacking Out — Systematic Desensitization of the Horse's Entire Body

Sacking out is the process of systematically exposing a horse to foreign objects — bags, tarps, ropes, flags, saddle pads — touching every part of its body until it accepts them without flinching. Done correctly it builds unshakeable confidence. Done wrong it creates trauma. The difference is method.

What Sacking Out Is — and What It Isn't

Sacking out is not flooding — it is not tying a horse to a post and throwing tarps at it until it gives up from exhaustion. True sacking out is systematic, progressive desensitization: introducing a stimulus at a level the horse can tolerate, waiting for the horse to relax and show acceptance, then gradually increasing intensity. The horse that is sacked out correctly becomes confident and curious. The horse that is flooded becomes shut down and unpredictably reactive — it looks quiet but has not actually processed the experience.

Warwick Schiller's polyvagal framework explains precisely why method matters here. A horse in a sympathetic (fight-or-flight) state cannot learn. Sacking out that pushes the horse past its threshold into panic shuts down the learning brain entirely. The goal is to work at the edge of the horse's comfort zone — present enough stimulus to be meaningful, not so much that the horse dissociates or shuts down. Signs the horse is processing correctly: licking and chewing, lowering the head, blinking, breathing out. Signs you've gone too far: eyes locked, body rigid, feet frozen — the horse has left the building mentally.

The Sequence — Body Parts and Objects

Clinton Anderson's sacking out sequence covers the horse's entire body systematically: neck, withers, back, hindquarters, belly, chest, and legs — each side. Begin with a soft rope or stick and string before introducing noisier or more dramatic objects like plastic bags, tarps, or flags. The horse that accepts a stick and string everywhere on its body is prepared for the saddle pad. The horse that accepts the saddle pad everywhere is prepared for the saddle. Each step builds on the last.

Key principle: do not move to a new body part or a new object until the horse is genuinely relaxed with the current one. Hurrying through areas the horse is uncomfortable with creates holes in the training that show up later as spooking, shying, or bucking under saddle.

Maintaining Sacking Out Through the Horse's Career

Horses that were well sacked out as youngsters can regress if they go long periods without desensitization work. A horse that has been off for six months and then encounters a flapping tarp at a show may react as if it has never seen one. Brief refresher sacking out sessions before competitions, trail rides, or new environments maintain the confidence that was built in early training.
