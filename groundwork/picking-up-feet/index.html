Picking Up Feet — Hoof Handling From First Touch to Farrier-Ready

A horse that picks up its feet willingly, holds them up calmly, and allows the hoof to be worked on without pulling away is worth its weight in gold to farriers, vets, and owners. A horse that fights hoof handling is a safety risk and an expensive problem. This skill starts on day one.

Why Hoof Handling Must Be Taught — Not Assumed

Many horse owners assume their horse will eventually accept hoof handling through repeated exposure with the farrier. This assumption costs money, causes injuries, and creates horses that get progressively worse at every trim. A horse that is genuinely trained to pick up its feet and hold them up willingly is a horse that stands quietly for the farrier, allows hoof picks and nippers without drama, and does not jeopardize the farrier's back, knees, or safety. That training happens on the owner's time — not on the farrier's clock.

Clinton Anderson is direct on this point: it is the horse owner's responsibility to train their horse to stand for the farrier, not the farrier's job to fight the horse into compliance at every appointment. A horse that the farrier cannot safely work on will eventually run out of farriers willing to take the call.

Starting From Zero — The First Foot

Begin with desensitization to touch on all four legs before attempting to pick up any foot. Run your hand down the leg, squeeze gently at the fetlock, and wait. Do not try to lift — just apply downward pressure on the fetlock and wait for the horse to shift its weight off that foot. The instant it does, release and reward. Repeat until the horse is rocking its weight off the foot predictably when you squeeze. Only then begin to ask for the foot to come up, supporting it gently and releasing as soon as it yields upward even slightly.

Building Hold Time and Tolerance

Once the horse picks up each foot, build the time it will hold it up willingly. Begin with one second, then two, then five, working up to the full time the farrier needs. Many horses pick up their feet readily but put them down before the farrier is ready — this is a training gap, not a physical problem. Ask for the foot, hold it for slightly longer than the horse wants to, then put it down before the horse takes it away. The horse learns that the foot comes down on your schedule, not its own.

The Reluctant Hind Foot

Hind feet present additional challenges because the horse cannot see what is happening behind it and the position is more vulnerable from a prey-animal perspective. Spend additional time desensitizing the hind legs to touch before attempting to pick them up, and always work methodically from high on the leg downward. A horse that kicks when its hind leg is touched has not been adequately desensitized to touch on the leg itself — the hoof handling comes after that work is complete, not instead of it.
