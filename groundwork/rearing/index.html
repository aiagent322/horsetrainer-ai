Rearing — Why It Happens and How to Fix It Safely

Rearing is one of the most dangerous behaviors a horse can develop. It is also one of the most misunderstood. Most horses that rear are not being defiant — they are telling you something is wrong: pain, fear, confusion, or too much pressure with no way out. Getting the diagnosis right determines whether the fix works.

🚨
Professional Help Is Strongly Recommended

A horse that rears with a rider is a genuine life-threatening danger. This page provides educational context. If your horse is rearing under saddle, please seek professional help before riding again.

Why Horses Rear

Warwick Schiller's analysis of rearing is one of the most instructive available. He distinguishes between two fundamentally different types of rearing: destination addiction rearing — the horse rears because it wants to go somewhere (home, other horses, the gate) and is being prevented from doing so — and pain/fear rearing — the horse rears because something in the environment or in the training is overwhelming and rearing is the only escape it can find. The treatment for each is completely different, and misidentifying which type you are dealing with will make the problem worse.

Clinton Anderson adds a third category: the horse that has learned through experience that rearing makes pressure go away. This is a trained behavior — the horse reared once, the rider released the reins and sat still, and the horse learned that rearing is effective. This type requires systematic retraining that removes the reward from the behavior.

The Pain Diagnosis

Before any training intervention for rearing, a veterinary examination is mandatory. Back pain, ulcers, bit fit issues, saddle fit problems, hock pain, and poll discomfort all produce rearing as a pain response. A horse that is rearing because it hurts will not improve through training — it will only get more desperate. Rule out pain first, every time, without exception.

Ground-Level Intervention

For horses that rear on the ground — when being led, when tied, when asked to move forward — the intervention begins from the ground before any mounted work is attempted. A horse that rears on the ground when asked to move forward has a forward-movement problem. Clinton Anderson's approach: make standing still and refusing to go forward more work than going forward, using lungeing and yielding exercises that require energy expenditure. The horse that discovers moving forward with the handler is the easy option quickly abandons rearing as a strategy.

When to Stop and Get Help

If a horse rears while you are riding it, dismount safely if possible and do not get back on until a professional has assessed both the horse and your riding. Rearing under saddle that is met with a grab for the mane, a rider that leans forward, or an unbalanced seat will reward the rear and escalate the behavior. This is not a problem for beginners or intermediate riders to solve alone.
